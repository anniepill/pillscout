1. VALUE
For a person asking about their own supplements and medicines, the service does not provide meaningful clinical value beyond what a current model with web search already delivers. In the evidence files (e.g., `evidence/cabinets/web_only/cabinet_a_model_a.md`), a model using only web search successfully identifies all major interactions: St. John’s wort with sertraline (serotonin syndrome) and warfarin, ginkgo with warfarin (bleeding risk), red yeast rice with atorvastatin (statin duplication), and levothyroxine absorption issues with calcium and iron. Furthermore, the web-search model correctly flags risks with ashwagandha and fish oil.

In contrast, the lookup-backed models (e.g., `evidence/cabinets/web_plus_lookup/model_a.md`) provide the same core warnings but miss ashwagandha and fish oil entirely because those ingredients are absent from the hand-authored corpus. What the service uniquely provides is byte-anchored, verbatim regulatory text (exact quotes from the EMA or FDA labels) and the preservation of multi-source disagreements (as seen in `evidence/single_questions/lookup_slices/model_b.md`).

However, a person at home does not need byte-anchored regulatory citations or IC50 values for CYP2C9 inhibition; they need synthesized, actionable safety warnings. The scorecards overstate the service's value for the end consumer. The consumer wants to know if they will bleed or suffer liver damage. Web-search models already synthesize this accurately and with broader coverage. The lookup service’s strict adherence to its limited corpus actually creates a false sense of security when it returns "no record" for common supplements that web search correctly flags as risky.

2. STOP OR CONTINUE
The strongest case for stopping:
The service, designed as a consumer-facing application, is redundant, incomplete, and dangerous. Modern LLMs equipped with web search already answer consumer interaction questions accurately, synthesizing actionable advice from authoritative sources like the FDA and NCCIH. The lookup service adds no clinical value for the end user; instead, it adds friction by surfacing dense regulatory text and conflicting studies. Worse, the service's coverage is severely limited by the manual authoring bottleneck. When it misses common supplements like ashwagandha or fish oil (as seen in the evidence files), it risks consumer harm through omission. The service also ignores prescription-to-prescription interactions (like omeprazole and clopidogrel), leaving glaring safety holes. The liability of a consumer relying on a "no record found" result is immense. Building and maintaining this hand-authored corpus is likely prohibitively expensive, and the end product solves a problem that general-purpose AI has already largely solved for the average person.

The strongest case for continuing:
The underlying infrastructure—the extraction pipeline and the method of proving a stored statement matches its source—is a highly valuable asset if repositioned. While consumers do not care about byte-anchored regulatory truth, enterprise healthcare and AI systems absolutely do. General-purpose LLMs are probabilistic and prone to hallucination, which is unacceptable in automated clinical decision-making. By creating a deterministic, verifiable corpus where every claim traces directly to a regulatory authority without synthesis or hallucination, this service provides a ground-truth API. The deterministic elemental math and proportional dosing capabilities represent structured logic that LLMs struggle to perform reliably. Continuing means pivoting away from a consumer chat app and instead selling the structured corpus and matching engine as a B2B infrastructure layer to pharmacies, health systems, or AI developers who need bulletproof, verifiable grounding for their own medical tools.

3. WHO IT IS FOR
If not a person at home, the service is for builders of health AI agents and pharmacies or health systems.

1. Builders of Health AI Agents
Developers building autonomous healthcare agents cannot rely on probabilistic web search because hallucinations in medical advice carry catastrophic risk. They would use the service’s extraction pipeline and byte-anchored corpus as a deterministic grounding layer (an API) for their LLMs. They would pay for API access to ensure every interaction claim their agent makes is traceable to a specific EMA monograph or FDA label.
What is missing: Comprehensive coverage. The corpus must scale rapidly to cover the long tail of supplements and must include prescription-to-prescription interactions to be a complete solution.

2. Pharmacies or Health Systems
Clinical pharmacists and health systems already have databases for prescription interactions (like First Databank), but they lack structured, verifiable data on dietary supplements. They would use the service to run deterministic elemental math on patient supplement intakes, flagging when a patient's combined cabinet exceeds upper limits for ingredients like calcium or vitamin D, or when a supplement duplicates a statin (e.g., red yeast rice). They would pay for EHR integration to protect patients and reduce liability.
What is missing: Real products resolved to their ingredients. Health systems need to map a specific barcode or brand name to its elemental components automatically, which is currently untested.

Repurposing the Built Assets
The core of what has been built—the structured records, the extraction pipeline, and the method of proving a stored statement matches its source—is worth vastly more used another way than as a consumer chat service. Consumers want synthesis; enterprises want verifiable data. The byte-anchored truth engine is a B2B infrastructure play. It should be decoupled from the consumer interface and sold as a "truth API" for medical data. By preserving multi-source disagreements rather than collapsing them, the service allows downstream enterprise software to apply its own risk thresholds, making the extraction pipeline a highly valuable tool for anyone building clinical decision support systems.

4. AGENTS
The evidence strongly suggests an AI agent needs this service much more than a person does. A human consumer can read a web-synthesized summary, apply common sense, and call their doctor to verify. An autonomous AI agent making decisions—such as adjusting a patient’s regimen, ordering refills, or generating clinical alerts—cannot rely on probabilistic web search. Hallucinations or missed nuances in medical data are fatal for autonomous systems.

Agents require deterministic, structured data. The service’s ability to provide byte-anchored, verbatim regulatory text ensures that an agent’s logic is grounded in verifiable truth. As seen in `evidence/single_questions/lookup_slices/model_b.md`, the service explicitly maps claims to specific classes and pathways (e.g., CYP2C9 inhibition), which an agent can use for deterministic reasoning.

For this to work, two things must be true. First, the software matching pipeline must be flawless, ensuring the agent never receives a false negative. Second, the cost of authoring a new ingredient must be low enough to achieve comprehensive coverage quickly, as an agent cannot function safely if its database is missing common supplements like ashwagandha.

5. LAW AND LIABILITY
Using this service as a consumer-facing application to analyze a user's specific cabinet likely falls under FDA regulations for Clinical Decision Support (CDS) software or Software as a Medical Device (SaMD). Because the service ingests patient-specific data (their cabinet) and outputs targeted medical warnings, it risks crossing the line into the practice of medicine or pharmacy. (I relied on internal knowledge of FDA CDS guidelines for this assessment).

The most severe liability arises when the service says "nothing found." As the evidence shows, the lookup service missed ashwagandha and fish oil because they were absent from the corpus, and it explicitly does not check prescription medicines against each other. If a consumer relies on this silence, assumes their combination is safe, and suffers a severe bleed or liver failure, the liability for the service provider is massive. A consumer cannot be expected to understand that "no record" simply means "not authored yet."

The safest uses avoid patient-specific diagnostic or treatment recommendations. The safest pivot is a B2B model where the service acts as a reference API for licensed clinicians or health systems. Under the FDA’s CDS exemptions, software is generally lower risk if it provides reference information and the healthcare professional can independently review the basis for the recommendations (which this service excels at via byte-anchoring). Providing raw, structured regulatory data to a pharmacy—where a human pharmacist makes the final clinical judgment—drastically reduces liability compared to giving incomplete safety assurances directly to a patient.

6. VERDICT
Change direction. Pivot immediately from a consumer-facing application to a B2B infrastructure API. The consumer service is redundant to web search, provides the wrong format of information (dense regulatory text instead of synthesized advice), and carries catastrophic liability for false negatives. However, the byte-anchored extraction pipeline is a highly valuable asset for grounding enterprise healthcare AI and clinical systems.

The one result that would change my mind and justify continuing the consumer app: proving that the service can achieve 100% comprehensive coverage of all supplements and prescriptions at near-zero cost, eliminating the "no record found" liability.

The cheapest test to produce this: Time-box the end-to-end authoring of 10 new, complex ingredients using the software matching pipeline. Measure the exact unit cost in time and dollars. If authoring remains slow and expensive, the consumer play is dead, and the B2B pivot is mandatory.

7. WHAT THIS PACKET GETS WRONG
The packet frames the lookup service as uniquely capable of finding critical interactions, leaving out the fact that current LLMs with web search already find the same major clinical warnings and often have better coverage (e.g., catching ashwagandha and fish oil). It frames byte-anchored regulatory citations and multi-source disagreements as a feature for consumers, when consumers actually just want synthesized, actionable safety advice.

Furthermore, it downplays the massive liability of "no record found" in a consumer setting, framing the absence of data as a mere limitation rather than a critical safety failure. Finally, it leaves out the immense, untested cost of manually authoring the corpus. By ignoring the unit cost of authoring a new ingredient and the difficulty of resolving real products to their elemental components, the packet frames the service's scalability in an overly optimistic light.
