**St John's wort is the clearest problem.** From your lookup record, the European herbal committee lists coumarin-type anticoagulants among the drugs that should not be used with St John's wort. Warfarin is that kind of anticoagulant. The same record says hyperforin in St John's wort speeds up enzymes that clear many drugs, so blood levels of those drugs can fall. With warfarin, a fall in effect can mean your blood is not thinned as intended. The same record also names sertraline directly: St John's wort can add to the serotonin effect of drugs such as sertraline, and a rare but serious reaction called serotonin syndrome can occur (sweating, fast heartbeat, diarrhea, fever, agitation, confusion, tremor). A U.S. drug label in the same record says combining St John's wort with SSRI antidepressants raises the risk of that syndrome. From the same record, atorvastatin was matched only by enzyme (CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein). The European text says to take special care with drugs cleared that way, and it gives simvastatin as an example, not atorvastatin. The practical meaning is that St John's wort can lower levels of statin drugs handled by that enzyme. I would stop St John's wort only after telling the prescriber, because stopping it can change warfarin levels in the other direction.

**Ginkgo with warfarin is disputed, and that is why it still needs the warfarin clinic.** From your lookup record, Health Canada says not to use ginkgo if you are taking blood thinners or other products that affect clotting. The U.S. warfarin label (Jantoven) says ginkgo is one of the botanicals that may cause bleeding on its own and that this effect would be expected to add to warfarin's, and it also lists ginkgo among botanicals that can interact with warfarin through liver enzymes. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says ginkgo may increase bleeding risk in people taking warfarin. The European herbal committee, in the same record, says available studies with warfarin do not indicate an interaction, and it still advises monitoring when you start ginkgo, change the dose, stop it, or switch products. A trial summary in that record found that a standard ginkgo extract did not significantly change clotting or warfarin's effect in healthy volunteers, and another study found no change in INR or platelet aggregation from ginkgo alone. The same European file also describes ginkgo constituents that inhibit platelet clumping in experiments, reports of bleeding, and a meta-analysis that did not find a higher bleeding risk for standardized ginkgo leaf extract. So the record is not a single verdict. What I would do with it: do not keep ginkgo going in the background of warfarin unless the prescriber knows and the INR is being watched. Health Canada's instruction is not to use the combination.

**Red yeast rice should not be taken with atorvastatin.** From your lookup record, European labeling says a red yeast rice product should not be consumed if you are taking cholesterol-lowering medication. The European Food Safety Authority states that monacolin K from red yeast rice is identical to lovastatin, a statin drug, and that it works by blocking the same cholesterol-making enzyme statins block. A later EFSA opinion in the record says intakes of monacolin K as low as 3 mg a day could lead to severe muscle effects, including rhabdomyolysis, and to liver effects. Taking red yeast rice beside atorvastatin is taking a second statin-type substance. Tell your prescriber you have been taking it. A small trial in adults 55 and older found that zolpidem impaired thinking, memory, and driving in the hours after a dose, and that prolonged-release melatonin made that impairment worse; the combination did not show a pharmacokinetic interaction. You already have valerian and passionflower on top of zolpidem, so melatonin is another sedative in the same pile. From outside the record, the U.S. label for prescription cannabidiol (Epidiolex) says cannabidiol is a moderate inhibitor of CYP2C19 and, for drugs such as clopidogrel whose effect depends on an active metabolite, it may lower that metabolite and reduce the drug's effect. That is the same enzyme problem as omeprazole, described for a prescription CBD product and its doses, not for whatever strength is in a shop-bought oil. It is still a reason to tell the prescriber you take CBD oil while you are on clopidogrel. That label also describes liver-enzyme rises with prescription cannabidiol. Supplement CBD is not the same product.

**Kava for anxiety: no, I would not start it.** From your lookup record, the World Health Organization says kava may potentiate centrally acting drugs such as alcohol, barbiturates, and other psychopharmacological agents. The European herbal committee says there is limited clinical evidence, and that kava preparations may interact with CNS depressants or psychoactive agents. A kava extract potentiated sedation and impaired coordination when combined with alcohol. There is a case report of coma after kava with alprazolam (a benzodiazepine; the kava dose was not given), and the clinical importance of a related case was not established. NIH LiverTox lists drowsiness among kava's common side effects. The World Health Organization says that even at recommended doses, kava may impair reflexes and the ability to drive or use machinery. You already take zolpidem, valerian, and passionflower, all of which the record ties to drowsiness. Adding kava puts another sedative on that stack. Separately, from outside the record, the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says kava products have been linked to rare but sometimes severe liver injury, including fatal cases, and that kava should not be used with other sedatives such as benzodiazepines or alcohol. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements page on kava summarizes the FDA consumer advisory of 25 March 2002: kava-containing supplements may be associated with severe liver injury, including hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver failure. The World Health Organization text in your lookup record also says kava is contraindicated in people with liver disease and in people with endogenous depression. Between the sedation you already have and the liver reports, I would leave kava on the shelf and ask the prescriber about the anxiety instead.

Vitamin C and zinc have no statement in the record against clopidogrel, amlodipine, omeprazole, or zolpidem, apart from the iron points above. No statement is not a finding of safety.
