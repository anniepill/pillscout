# 1. I take warfarin. Can I take ginkgo?

ANSWER:

These records do not support adding ginkgo to warfarin on your own. They also do not all say the same thing.

Health Canada says not to use ginkgo if you are taking blood thinners or other health products that affect blood coagulation.

The European Medicines Agency says that if ginkgo is taken with anticoagulants (it names phenprocoumon and warfarin) or with antiplatelet drugs, their effect may be influenced. For people who already have a pathologically increased bleeding tendency, and for people on anticoagulant or antiplatelet treatment, it says the product should be used only after consultation with a doctor. It also says ginkgo preparations might increase susceptibility to bleeding, and that the product should be stopped as a precaution 3 to 4 days before surgery. Bleeding from individual organs (eye, nose, brain, and gut) has been reported, and the frequency is not known. In the traditional-use column, the same monograph says ginkgo leaf may increase the effects of anticoagulants such as coumarin derivatives.

That European monograph also says available studies with warfarin do not indicate an interaction between warfarin and Ginkgo biloba products, but that adequate monitoring is advised when starting ginkgo, when changing the ginkgo dose, when stopping ginkgo, or when changing product. A study recounted in the European assessment report found that the international normalised ratio of prothrombin time and platelet aggregation were not affected when ginkgo was taken with warfarin, and it concluded that the extract EGb 761 at recommended doses does not significantly affect clotting status or the pharmacodynamics of warfarin in healthy subjects. A related report says INR and platelet aggregation were not affected by ginkgo or ginger alone.

In laboratory work cited in the ginkgo record, the constituent quercetin strongly inhibited CYP2C9, and the source says that enzyme clears some drugs with a narrow range, such as S-warfarin. It says the importance of that laboratory finding still has to be evaluated in clinical studies. A study in healthy people suggested ginkgo did not affect CYP2C9 activity.

The U.S. warfarin label (Jantoven) says some botanicals may cause bleeding when taken alone, and it names garlic and Ginkgo biloba. It says they may have anticoagulant, antiplatelet, and/or fibrinolytic properties, and that those effects would be expected to add to the anticoagulant effects of Jantoven. The same label lists ginkgo among botanicals that can interact with Jantoven through CYP450 interactions.

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding in people who are taking anticoagulant drugs such as warfarin.

What this adds up to: one regulator says do not use ginkgo with blood thinners. The warfarin label expects added anticoagulant effect. The European text says the anticoagulant effect may be influenced, asks for a doctor if you are already on an anticoagulant, and asks for monitoring when ginkgo is started, the dose changes, it is stopped, or the product changes, while also recording studies in healthy people that did not show a change in INR or clotting status.

# 2. Is ginkgo safe during pregnancy?

ANSWER:

The records do not describe ginkgo as safe in pregnancy.

For the well-established-use dry extract, the European Medicines Agency lists pregnancy as a contraindication. The pregnancy text says Ginkgo biloba extracts may impair the ability of platelets to aggregate, the tendency for bleeding may be increased, animal studies are insufficient with respect to reproductive toxicity, and use is contraindicated in pregnancy.

For traditional use, the same monograph says safety during pregnancy and lactation has not been established, and that in the absence of sufficient data use during pregnancy and lactation is not recommended.

Health Canada says to ask a health care practitioner before use if you are pregnant or breastfeeding.

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says ginkgo may be unsafe for use during pregnancy. It says ginkgo might cause early labor or extra bleeding during delivery if used near that time. It also says little is known about whether it is safe to use ginkgo while breastfeeding.

# 3. What does ginkgo do to blood clotting, and how?

ANSWER:

The European monograph says the exact mechanism is not known. The human data it cites include a reduction in blood viscosity and a reduction in platelet aggregation, along with changes in brain perfusion and blood flow that are not themselves clotting effects.

A separate mechanism in the record is antagonism of platelet-activating factor (PAF). The ginkgolides, and in particular ginkgolide B, are described as antagonists of PAF. PAF is described as a potent inducer of platelet aggregation. In laboratory work, PAF-mediated aggregation of human platelets was inhibited by ginkgolides A, B, and C and by the extract EGb 761, and ginkgolide B had the strongest antagonistic effect of the ginkgolides tested. Purified ginkgolides A, B, and C, described as potent PAF-receptor antagonists, inhibited PAF-induced platelet aggregation and did not inhibit oxidant-induced aggregation. The European pregnancy text ties this to bleeding: the extracts may impair the ability of platelets to aggregate, and the tendency for bleeding may be increased. The warnings text says preparations containing ginkgo might increase susceptibility to bleeding.

The clinical bleeding evidence in the same record is mixed. Case reports and a German pharmacovigilance count associate ginkgo with bleeding in several organs, and the monograph lists bleeding of individual organs as an undesirable effect of unknown frequency. A meta-analysis of haemostasis outcomes did not indicate a higher bleeding risk for standardised ginkgo leaf extract compared with placebo. In a large trial, rates of major bleeding did not differ between ginkgo and placebo. In healthy subjects, EGb 761 at recommended doses did not significantly affect clotting status when taken with warfarin, and INR and platelet aggregation were not affected by ginkgo alone.

A separate laboratory finding is not a direct effect on the clotting proteins: quercetin from ginkgo inhibited CYP2C9, the enzyme the source says clears S-warfarin. That is about how the body clears a drug, and the source says it still needs clinical evaluation. A study in healthy people suggested ginkgo did not change CYP2C9 activity.

# 4. I'm on birth control pills. Does St John's wort matter?

ANSWER:

Yes. For the well-established use of St John's wort, the European Medicines Agency says the reduction of plasma concentrations of hormonal contraceptives may lead to increased intermenstrual bleeding and reduced safety in birth control. It says women using hormonal contraceptives should take additional contraceptive measures.

The mechanism in the record is pharmacokinetic and dose-dependent. Hyperforin induces the enzymes CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19, and the transporter P-glycoprotein, through the PXR system. Because of that, elimination of other drugs may be accelerated and their plasma concentrations may fall. The record puts the induction threshold above 1 mg of hyperforin a day. The same interaction section says special care is needed with drugs whose metabolism is influenced by those enzymes or by P-glycoprotein, because a reduction of plasma concentrations is possible. Elevated enzyme activity returns to normal within 1 week after stopping.

There is a low-dose exception in the traditional-use column. If the daily intake of hyperforin is less than 1 mg and the duration is not longer than 2 weeks, the monograph says no clinically relevant interactions are reported for drugs metabolised by those enzymes or transported by P-glycoprotein. Above 1 mg of hyperforin a day, the full induction profile applies. Birth-control pills sit in the group the well-established-use text warns about, so at those doses St John's wort can make the pill less reliable, and the source's instruction is to use an additional contraceptive measure.

# 5. How much zinc per day is too much?

ANSWER:

These records answer that with a tolerable upper intake level, and two authorities in the file set different numbers. Both levels are for healthy people and are based on zinc's adverse effect on copper status. Above the level is the amount the source treats as too much. The file does not reconcile the two figures.

The U.S. Food and Nutrition Board levels, as given on the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements zinc fact sheet, are total zinc from food and supplements:

- 0–6 months: 4 mg a day
- 7–12 months: 5 mg a day
- 1–3 years: 7 mg a day
- 4–8 years: 12 mg a day
- 9–13 years: 23 mg a day
- 14–18 years, including pregnancy and lactation: 34 mg a day
- 19 years and older, including pregnancy and lactation: 40 mg a day

The fact sheet says these upper levels do not apply to people who are receiving zinc for medical treatment, and that those people should be under the care of a physician.

The European Scientific Committee on Food set a lower adult figure: 25 mg a day, including pregnant and lactating women. That opinion derives it from a no-observed-adverse-effect level of 50 mg a day on copper-status indicators, divided by an uncertainty factor of 2. For children and adolescents it extrapolated from the adult level: 7 mg (1–3 years), 10 mg (4–6), 13 mg (7–10), 18 mg (11–14), and 22 mg (15–17). The record also says the 2014 European Food Safety Authority opinion on zinc reference values sets no upper level; the European upper level in this file is the earlier Scientific Committee on Food opinion.

The same fact sheet describes what high intakes do. High zinc intakes can cause nausea, dizziness, headaches, gastric distress, vomiting, and loss of appetite. If used for weeks, doses of 50 mg or more, typically from supplements or from heavy use of zinc-containing denture adhesive, can interfere with copper absorption, cause low copper status, reduce immune function, and lower HDL cholesterol. Overuse of denture creams containing up to 34 mg of zinc per gram has been reported to lead to neurological symptoms, including sensory ataxia and myelopathy, and anemia.

Health Canada's multi-vitamin monograph is a product-formulation range, and the record says it is distinct from the Food and Nutrition Board upper level and that the two are not reconciled. For zinc from non-picolinate sources, the monograph maximum for people 19 and older is 50 mg a day. For zinc picolinate, the adult maximum is 25 mg a day. A product that provides more than 40 mg of zinc a day must carry a specific use or purpose statement. Those maxima are not the tolerable upper intake level.

For a healthy adult, the two upper levels in this file are 40 mg a day (United States) and 25 mg a day (European Scientific Committee on Food). Intake above the level that applies to you is what these records call too much.

# 6. Does ginkgo interact with metformin?

ANSWER:

The ginkgo record has one direct study of metformin, and it is a null finding on pharmacokinetics. Coingestion of 120 mg of the extract EGb 761 and 500 mg of metformin did not significantly affect the pharmacokinetic properties of metformin.

The same record has a broader caution that is not a metformin-specific finding. Health Canada says to ask a health care practitioner before use if you are taking diabetes medications. Metformin is a diabetes medication, so that caution covers it as a class, without describing an effect on metformin itself.

No other statement in the ginkgo record reports a change in metformin levels, effect, or safety. The pharmacokinetic study above is the only metformin-specific result, and it did not find a significant pharmacokinetic interaction at those doses.

# 8. I take ginkgo, St John's wort, zinc and warfarin. What should I know?

ANSWER:

The combination that these records speak to most directly is warfarin with ginkgo and warfarin with St John's wort. They describe different problems, and neither one is something the files say to manage on your own. Zinc is also in the stack, but the zinc record does not document an interaction with warfarin, ginkgo, or St John's wort.

Warfarin and ginkgo. Health Canada says not to use ginkgo with blood thinners or other products that affect blood coagulation. The European monograph says that if ginkgo is taken with anticoagulants, and it names warfarin, their effect may be influenced, and that people on anticoagulant treatment should use ginkgo only after consultation with a doctor. It says ginkgo might increase susceptibility to bleeding and should be stopped 3 to 4 days before surgery. It also says available warfarin studies do not indicate an interaction, but that monitoring is advised when ginkgo is started, the dose is changed, ginkgo is stopped, or the product is changed. A study of EGb 761 at recommended doses in healthy subjects did not find a significant effect on clotting status or on the pharmacodynamics of warfarin. The U.S. warfarin label says ginkgo may cause bleeding on its own and may have anticoagulant, antiplatelet, and/or fibrinolytic properties, and that those effects would be expected to add to warfarin's effect. The U.S. complementary-health summary says ginkgo may increase bleeding risk in people on anticoagulants such as warfarin. How ginkgo can affect clotting is covered in question 3: it can reduce platelet aggregation, including through ginkgolide antagonism of platelet-activating factor, and the exact overall mechanism is stated as unknown.

Warfarin and St John's wort. The St John's wort record contraindicates concomitant use with coumarin-type anticoagulants when the daily hyperforin dose is above 1 mg. That contraindication is for the well-established use and for the high-dose traditional-use tier. It does not print the word warfarin in that sentence. The warfarin label quoted in the ginkgo record does: it lists St John's wort among botanicals that can interact with warfarin (Jantoven) through CYP450 interactions, in the same sentence that names ginkgo. The mechanism in the St John's wort record is enzyme induction, not an added bleeding effect. Hyperforin induces CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and P-glycoprotein, dose-dependently, above 1 mg of hyperforin a day, and the elimination of other drugs may be accelerated so that plasma concentrations fall. The ginkgo record says CYP2C9 is the enzyme that clears S-warfarin. Put next to the St John's wort induction statement, that is a reason the files flag the pair: St John's wort can speed clearance through an enzyme that handles S-warfarin, which is a concentration problem, while ginkgo is described as a possible added effect on clotting and bleeding. Those are not the same direction. Enzyme activity is said to return to normal within a week after St John's wort is stopped. If daily hyperforin is under 1 mg and use is no longer than 2 weeks, the European traditional-use text says no clinically relevant interactions are reported for drugs metabolised by those enzymes; the warfarin contraindication language in this record is the high-hyperforin one.

Zinc. The zinc record's documented partners are high-dose iron, very high-dose zinc with magnesium, penicillamine, quinolone antibiotics, tetracycline antibiotics, thiazide diuretics, and a general instruction about other oral medicines. Warfarin, ginkgo, and St John's wort are not among them. Health Canada does say that products providing zinc should be taken a few hours before or after other medications or health products. The upper levels in question 5 still apply to the zinc itself: for a healthy adult, 40 mg a day on the U.S. Food and Nutrition Board figure and 25 mg a day on the European Scientific Committee on Food figure, with copper-status harm described at 50 mg a day for weeks.

What to take from the four together is that ginkgo and St John's wort are each flagged with warfarin, for different reasons, and the zinc file adds a timing separation rather than a warfarin interaction. The European ginkgo text asks for monitoring when ginkgo starts, the dose changes, it stops, or the product changes. The St John's wort text treats coumarin-type anticoagulants as contraindicated above 1 mg of hyperforin a day. The warfarin label names both ginkgo and St John's wort.
