# Supplement and Medication Questions

## 1. I take warfarin. Can I take ginkgo?

### ANSWER:
No, you should not take ginkgo biloba while taking warfarin without consulting your healthcare provider, and in general, this combination is strongly advised against.

Warfarin is a powerful prescription anticoagulant (blood thinner) that prevents blood clots by slowing down your body's production of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors. Ginkgo biloba also has blood-thinning properties, but it acts primarily as an antiplatelet agent—preventing platelets (the tiny cell fragments in your blood) from clumping together to form a clot.

When you take warfarin and ginkgo together, their effects add up. This significantly increases your risk of serious and potentially life-threatening bleeding complications, such as severe gastrointestinal bleeding or bleeding in the brain (intracranial hemorrhage). Additionally, herbal supplements can vary in potency and may unpredictably alter how your liver processes warfarin.

If you are already taking ginkgo alongside warfarin, do not stop or change anything abruptly on your own; contact your prescribing doctor or anticoagulation clinic right away so they can safely check your INR (blood clotting time) and guide you. Seek immediate medical attention if you notice any warning signs of bleeding, such as unusual bruising, frequent nosebleeds, bleeding gums, pink or red urine, black or tarry stools, or sudden severe headaches.

- Antiplatelet action and increased bleeding risk of Ginkgo biloba: National Institutes of Health (NIH), National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), *"Ginkgo" Herbs at a Glance*.

## 2. Is ginkgo safe during pregnancy?

### ANSWER:
No, ginkgo biloba is not considered safe during pregnancy and should be avoided.

There are two major safety concerns:
1. **Risk of severe bleeding:** Ginkgo inhibits platelet aggregation (blood clotting). During pregnancy, labor, delivery, and the postpartum period, this antiplatelet effect can significantly increase the risk of maternal hemorrhage or bleeding complications for the newborn. Medical guidelines advise stopping ginkgo at least two to three weeks prior to any planned surgery or anticipated delivery.
2. **Potential uterine stimulation:** Preliminary laboratory and animal research suggests that components of ginkgo extract may stimulate uterine contractions, which could potentially trigger premature labor or complications.

In addition, there is a lack of rigorous clinical trial data establishing fetal safety. If you are pregnant or trying to conceive, you should stop taking ginkgo and speak with your obstetrician or midwife about any supplements or remedies you take.

### SOURCES:

## 3. What does ginkgo do to blood clotting, and how?

### ANSWER:
Ginkgo biloba reduces your blood's ability to clot—acting as a mild "blood thinner" (specifically an antiplatelet agent)—which prolongs bleeding time and inhibits thrombus (clot) formation.

The biological mechanisms by which it does this include:
- **Inhibition of Platelet-Activating Factor (PAF):** In normal blood clotting, damaged blood vessel walls trigger platelets to clump together (primary hemostasis). Platelet-activating factor (PAF) is a potent phospholipid signaling molecule that binds to platelet surface receptors, causing platelets to change shape, release internal clotting chemicals, and aggregate. Ginkgo biloba extract contains specialized terpene lactones known as ginkgolides (especially Ginkgolide B). Ginkgolide B is a potent, selective, competitive antagonist of the PAF receptor. By occupying the PAF receptor, it prevents PAF from triggering platelet aggregation.
- **Endothelial Vasodilation and Nitric Oxide Stimulation:** Ginkgo also contains flavonoid glycosides (such as quercetin, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin) and bilobalide. These compounds stimulate vascular endothelial cells to produce and release nitric oxide and prostacyclin, which promote blood vessel relaxation and further inhibit platelet activation.
- **Coagulation Cascade Distinction:** Ginkgo does not directly inhibit the enzymatic coagulation cascade (the sequence of clotting factor proteins like prothrombin and fibrinogen targeted by drugs like heparin or direct oral anticoagulants). Instead, it targets platelet function; however, because platelet plug formation is essential to establish a stable clot, impairing platelets compromises overall hemostasis and prolongs bleeding time.

- Pharmacodynamics, constituents (terpene lactones, ginkgolides, flavonoids), and antiplatelet mechanisms: European Medicines Agency (EMA), Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC), *Assessment report on Ginkgo biloba L., folium* (EMA/HMPC/321095/2012).

## 4. I'm on birth control pills. Does St John's wort matter?

### ANSWER:
Yes, St. John's wort matters tremendously. Taking St. John's wort while taking birth control pills can cause your birth control to fail, leading to breakthrough bleeding and unintended pregnancy. You should not take St. John's wort if you rely on hormonal contraception.

Here is how the interaction occurs:
Birth control pills work by delivering synthetic hormones (estrogen and progestin) into your bloodstream to prevent ovulation. Your body breaks down and clears these hormones primarily through a liver enzyme system known as Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) and an intestinal transport protein called P-glycoprotein.

St. John's wort (*Hypericum perforatum*) contains an active constituent called hyperforin, which is a powerful inducer of both CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein. When you take St. John's wort, it signals your liver to produce significantly larger quantities of these metabolizing enzymes. As a result, your liver breaks down and eliminates the contraceptive hormones far faster than normal. Hormone levels drop below the therapeutic threshold required to block ovulation, allowing your ovaries to release an egg.

What you should do:
- If you are currently taking St. John's wort, consult your doctor about stopping it safely.
- Immediately start using a reliable, non-hormonal backup method of birth control (such as barrier methods/condoms).
- Continue using backup protection for at least 4 weeks after discontinuing St. John's wort, because it takes several weeks for induced liver enzymes to return to their normal baseline activity.

### SOURCES:
- Public health advisory on St. John's wort decreasing oral contraceptive effectiveness: U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), *FDA Public Health Advisory: Risk of Drug Interactions with St. John's Wort and Other Medications* (February 2000).
- Drug safety update on unintended pregnancies and hormonal contraceptive failure with St John's wort: Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), *Drug Safety Update: St John's wort: interaction with hormonal contraceptives, including implants – reduced contraceptive efficacy and unintended pregnancies* (March 2014).
- Pharmacokinetic mechanism of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein induction by hyperforin: European Medicines Agency (EMA), Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC), *Community herbal monograph on Hypericum perforatum L., herba* (EMA/HMPC/1119/2005).

## 5. How much zinc per day is too much?

### ANSWER:
For adults (aged 19 and older), the established Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) for zinc is **40 milligrams (mg) per day**. This 40 mg daily limit includes all zinc combined—from your diet, fortified foods, and any dietary supplements, multivitamins, or cold lozenges. Consuming more than 40 mg daily on a routine basis is considered too much and can cause toxic health effects.

For infants and children, the upper limits are substantially lower:
- Ages 1 to 3: 7 mg/day
- Ages 4 to 8: 12 mg/day
- Ages 9 to 13: 23 mg/day
- Ages 14 to 18: 34 mg/day

Why excess zinc is dangerous:
1. **Copper Deficiency (Chronic Toxicity):** Zinc and copper compete for the same absorption pathways in the small intestine. Excess zinc stimulates intestinal cells to produce an intracellular protein called metallothionein, which binds tightly to copper and prevents its absorption. Taking 50 mg or more of zinc daily over weeks or months frequently leads to severe copper deficiency, which causes:
   - Hematologic disorders: microcytic or normocytic anemia and neutropenia (low white blood cell count, worsening immune defense).
   - Neurological damage: sensory ataxia, peripheral neuropathy, and myelopathy (spinal cord dysfunction leading to numbness, weakness, and difficulty walking), which can be permanent if not caught early.
2. **Acute Gastrointestinal Distress:** Ingesting large single doses of zinc (e.g., 50 to 150 mg or more, common in high-dose supplements or zinc lozenges) causes acute nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, diarrhea, and a persistent metallic taste.
3. **Immune Suppression and Lipid Alterations:** While adequate zinc supports the immune system, chronic excess zinc paradoxically impairs immune cell function and can lower HDL ("good") cholesterol levels.

For context, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA)—what your body actually requires—is only 11 mg per day for adult men and 8 mg per day for adult women (11–12 mg during pregnancy or lactation). Most individuals meet their daily requirements through food alone.

### SOURCES:
- Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (UL) and Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA) for zinc: Institute of Medicine (IOM), Food and Nutrition Board, National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *Dietary Reference Intakes for Vitamin A, Vitamin K, Arsenic, Boron, Chromium, Copper, Iodine, Iron, Manganese, Molybdenum, Nickel, Silicon, Vanadium, and Zinc* (2001, Chapter 12: Zinc).
- Zinc toxicity, copper deficiency mechanisms, anemia, and neurological manifestations: National Institutes of Health (NIH), Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS), *Zinc: Fact Sheet for Health Professionals*.
- European dietary reference values and maximum tolerable intake: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Scientific Committee on Food / Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies, *Tolerable Upper Intake Levels for Vitamins and Minerals*.

## 6. Does ginkgo interact with metformin?

### ANSWER:
Yes, ginkgo biloba can interact with metformin, primarily by altering blood sugar regulation and how your body handles insulin and metformin.

Key aspects of this interaction include:
1. **Changes in Blood Glucose Control:** Metformin is prescribed to manage type 2 diabetes by lowering hepatic glucose production, decreasing intestinal absorption of glucose, and improving insulin sensitivity. Clinical studies have shown that ginkgo biloba extract can alter pancreatic beta-cell insulin secretion and hepatic clearance of insulin and glucose. In people taking metformin, adding ginkgo can cause unpredictable changes in glycemic control—some clinical research has documented blunted metformin efficacy leading to higher blood sugar spikes, while other data suggest unexpected drops in blood sugar.
2. **Transporter and Elimination Effects:** Preliminary pharmacokinetic research indicates that ginkgo flavonoids may modulate organic cation transporters (such as OCT1 and OCT2), which are responsible for the cellular transport and renal excretion of metformin, potentially altering metformin distribution in the body.
3. **Bleeding Risk in Diabetes:** Patients with diabetes frequently have microvascular fragility (such as diabetic retinopathy). Because ginkgo inhibits platelet aggregation, taking it alongside diabetes therapies requires extra caution to avoid retinal or other bleeding complications.

What you should do:
Talk to the doctor managing your diabetes before taking ginkgo biloba. If your physician approves taking both, you should monitor your blood glucose levels more frequently and stay alert for symptoms of hypoglycemia (such as sweating, shakiness, dizziness, hunger, and palpitations) or unexplained hyperglycemia.

## 7. Is ashwagandha safe with thyroid medication?

### ANSWER:
No, ashwagandha is generally not considered safe to take with thyroid medication, and doing so is discouraged without direct medical supervision.

Here is why:
1. **Thyroid Hormone Stimulation:** Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) has been shown in clinical trials and pharmacological studies to stimulate the thyroid gland. It promotes the synthesis and release of thyroid hormones, significantly increasing circulating levels of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), while suppressing thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH).
2. **Interaction with Thyroid Replacement (e.g., Levothyroxine / Synthroid):** When treating an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), medication dosages are finely tuned to restore normal hormone balance. Adding ashwagandha creates an additive effect that can push your hormone levels into an abnormally high range, triggering drug-induced hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis). This can cause serious symptoms, including rapid or irregular heartbeats (tachycardia, palpitations, atrial fibrillation), chest tightness, tremors, nervousness, anxiety, insomnia, heat intolerance, and accelerated bone mineral loss.
3. **Interaction with Antithyroid Medications (e.g., Methimazole, Propylthiouracil):** If you take medication for an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism), ashwagandha counteracts your medication by promoting thyroid hormone production.

What you should do:
Do not take ashwagandha if you are on thyroid medication. If you are already taking both, contact your prescribing physician or endocrinologist promptly. They will likely need to perform a thyroid function panel (TSH, free T4, free T3) to assess your hormone levels and ensure your medication dosage remains safe.

### SOURCES:
- Clinical trial demonstrating ashwagandha increases T3 and T4 levels and reduces TSH: Sharma, A. - Adverse event reports and warnings regarding ashwagandha-induced thyrotoxicosis and interactions with thyroid hormone therapy: Therapeutic Research Center, *Natural Medicines Comprehensive Database* (Monograph: Ashwagandha, section on Interactions with Drugs: Thyroid Hormone).

## 8. I take ginkgo, St John's wort, zinc and warfarin. What should I know?

### ANSWER:
You should know that this combination is extremely dangerous and potentially life-threatening. You should contact your prescribing doctor, cardiologist, or anticoagulation clinic immediately. **Do not abruptly stop taking these medications or supplements on your own today without professional medical supervision**, because sudden changes can trigger acute complications.

Here is why this combination is a critical health hazard:

1. **Warfarin + St. John's Wort (High Risk of Blood Clots and Stroke):**
   - Warfarin is a narrow-therapeutic-index drug, meaning minor shifts in blood concentration can cause treatment failure or severe hemorrhage. Warfarin is metabolized primarily by liver enzymes Cytochrome P450 2C9 (CYP2C9) and CYP3A4.
   - St. John's wort is a potent inducer of CYP2C9 and CYP3A4. It causes your liver to rapidly clear warfarin from your blood, causing your INR to plummet.
   - When your INR drops below therapeutic range, your blood clots too easily, putting you at immediate risk for a stroke, heart attack, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), or pulmonary embolism.
   - **Critical caution:** If you abruptly stop taking St. John's wort, the enzyme induction will fade, warfarin blood levels will spike dramatically, and you could suffer massive, uncontrolled bleeding. Discontinuation must be medically supervised.

2. **Warfarin + Ginkgo Biloba (Major Bleeding Hazard):**
   - Ginkgo biloba contains ginkgolides that block platelet-activating factor (PAF), preventing platelets from aggregating (antiplatelet action).
   - Warfarin inhibits the formation of secondary clotting factors.
   - Combining an antiplatelet herb with an anticoagulant creates an additive, synergistic bleeding risk. This can lead to severe internal bleeding, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, or hemorrhagic stroke (bleeding in the brain).
   - Crucially, routine INR blood tests only measure clotting factors—they do not measure platelet function. Your INR could appear low or normal while your platelets are severely inhibited, creating a false sense of security.

3. **Volatile Clotting and Bleeding Tug-of-War:**
   - St. John's wort works to neutralize warfarin's anticoagulant action, while ginkgo actively disables your platelets. This unstable combination makes safe anticoagulation nearly impossible and leaves you simultaneously vulnerable to fatal blood clots and fatal hemorrhages.

4. **Zinc Considerations:**
   - If your daily zinc intake exceeds 40 mg/day, it can cause chronic copper deficiency, anemia, and immune dysfunction. In addition, high-dose mineral supplements can bind other substances in the digestive tract and interfere with absorption.

What you should do right now:
- Contact your doctor or anticoagulation clinic today. They will create a safe plan to gradually taper or stop the supplements, monitor your INR every few days, and adjust your warfarin dose until your blood stabilizes.
- Go to an emergency room immediately if you experience warning signs of bleeding (severe sudden headache, coughing or vomiting blood, black or bloody stools, pink or red urine, severe dizziness) or warning signs of a clot (sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, swelling or pain in one leg, sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking).

### SOURCES:
- Boxed warning and drug interaction advisory on CYP2C9/CYP3A4 inducers (St. John's wort) reducing warfarin levels, and antiplatelet herbs increasing bleeding risk: U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) / Bristol-Myers Squibb, *Coumadin (warfarin sodium) Prescribing Information*.
- Co-administration hazards of St. John's wort and Ginkgo biloba with anticoagulants: National Institutes of Health (NIH), National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), *"St. John's Wort"* and *"Ginkgo"* Fact Sheets.
- Clinical guidelines on managing herb-drug interactions in anticoagulated patients: American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP), *Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines on Antithrombotic Therapy*.
- Safety alerts on St John's wort-induced subtherapeutic INR and thrombosis risk: Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), *Drug Safety Advice on St John's wort interactions with warfarin*.
- Zinc Upper Intake Levels and copper deficiency risks: Institute of Medicine (IOM), *Dietary Reference Intakes for Zinc* (2001).

## 9. I take amoxicillin. Can I take ginkgo?

### ANSWER:
In general, there is no known direct pharmacokinetic drug interaction between amoxicillin and ginkgo biloba. Amoxicillin does not interfere with the active compounds in ginkgo, and ginkgo does not prevent amoxicillin from effectively treating your bacterial infection.

However, there are several clinical considerations to keep in mind:

1. **Overlapping Gastrointestinal Side Effects:**
   - Both amoxicillin and ginkgo biloba commonly cause digestive side effects, including nausea, stomach cramps, diarrhea, and vomiting. Taking them together may increase the likelihood or severity of an upset stomach. Taking amoxicillin with meals can help reduce stomach irritation.
2. **Context of Infection and Minor Bleeding:**
   - If you were prescribed amoxicillin following surgery, a dental extraction, or for an infection involving an open wound, remember that ginkgo biloba has mild blood-thinning (antiplatelet) properties. This could increase minor bleeding, oozing, or bruising at the wound site.
3. **Allergic Reactions and Diagnostic Confusion:**
   - Amoxicillin is a common cause of drug allergies (producing symptoms like skin rashes, hives, or itching). Ginkgo biloba can also occasionally cause allergic skin reactions or headaches. Starting ginkgo at the same time as a new antibiotic makes it harder to identify the culprit if an allergic reaction occurs.
4. **General Recommendation:**
   - When treating an acute bacterial infection, it is best to complete your prescribed course of amoxicillin before starting new herbal supplements. Always ensure your prescribing doctor or pharmacist is aware of all vitamins, herbs, and supplements you take.

- Pharmacokinetics and drug interaction profile of amoxicillin (renal elimination via glomerular filtration and tubular secretion, lack of CYP-mediated interactions): U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), *Amoxil (amoxicillin) Prescribing Information*.
- Interaction monograph confirming no documented direct clinical interaction between aminopenicillins and Ginkgo biloba: Therapeutic Research Center, *Natural Medicines Comprehensive Database* (Monograph: Ginkgo Biloba).
