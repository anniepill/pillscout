# 1. I take warfarin. Can I take ginkgo?

**ANSWER:** I would avoid starting ginkgo unless the clinician who manages your warfarin specifically approves it. Ginkgo may make bleeding more likely, and combining it with an anticoagulant such as warfarin is a potentially serious risk even though research has not measured the size of that risk consistently. Do not stop or change your warfarin on your own. If you already take ginkgo, tell your anticoagulation clinic or pharmacist promptly; they can decide whether you should stop it and whether your INR needs checking. Get urgent help for unusual or persistent bleeding, black or bloody stools, vomiting or coughing blood, a severe sudden headache, marked weakness, or a significant blow to the head.

**SOURCES:**
- National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), *Ginkgo: Usefulness and Safety*: ginkgo may increase bleeding risk in people taking anticoagulants such as warfarin, and supplement–medicine interactions should be discussed with a health professional.
- European Medicines Agency (EMA), Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products, *European Union herbal monograph on Ginkgo biloba L., folium* and its assessment report: cautions about use with anticoagulant and antiplatelet medicines and bleeding tendency.
- U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), *Coumadin (warfarin sodium) Prescribing Information*, sections on warnings/precautions and drug interactions: bleeding is warfarin's major hazard; patients should not alter therapy without medical guidance and need monitoring when interacting products change.

# 2. Is ginkgo safe during pregnancy?

**ANSWER:** Ginkgo should generally be avoided during pregnancy. There is not enough good evidence to establish that it is safe, and it may increase bleeding risk—an especially important concern around labor and delivery. If you have already taken it, that does not by itself mean harm occurred, but stop and discuss the product, dose, and timing with your obstetric clinician or pharmacist. Seek care promptly for vaginal bleeding or other concerning symptoms.

**SOURCES:**
- NCCIH, *Ginkgo: Usefulness and Safety*: ginkgo may be unsafe during pregnancy because it might cause early labor or extra bleeding during delivery; little is known about its safety while breastfeeding.
- EMA, Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products, *European Union herbal monograph on Ginkgo biloba L., folium*: use during pregnancy is contraindicated because ginkgo preparations may impair platelet aggregation and because reproductive-safety data are inadequate.

# 3. What does ginkgo do to blood clotting, and how?

**ANSWER:** Ginkgo can reduce platelet activity. Platelets are blood cells that stick together to help form an initial clot, so suppressing their activity can make bleeding easier or longer. A proposed mechanism is antagonism of platelet-activating factor by ginkgolides, constituents of ginkgo extracts. Laboratory findings and case reports support a bleeding concern, but human trials have been inconsistent; it is not accurate to say that every standardized dose predictably “thins the blood” by a known amount. Ginkgo does not work like warfarin and is not a substitute for an anticoagulant.

**SOURCES:**
- EMA, Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products, *Assessment report on Ginkgo biloba L., folium*: describes inhibition of platelet aggregation/platelet-activating factor effects, bleeding reports, and the limitations and inconsistency of the clinical evidence.
- NCCIH, *Ginkgo: Usefulness and Safety*: identifies bleeding as a potential risk, particularly with anticoagulants.
- The statement that ginkgo is not a substitute for prescribed anticoagulation: no source I can name.

# 4. I'm on birth control pills. Does St John's wort matter?

**ANSWER:** Yes. St John's wort can speed up the enzymes and transport systems that clear many medicines, including hormones in birth-control pills. That can lower hormone exposure, cause breakthrough bleeding, and make contraception less reliable. Do not rely on the pill alone while taking St John's wort. Contact a pharmacist or contraceptive clinician now for advice on stopping St John's wort and using a method unaffected by enzyme induction, or on how long backup contraception is needed after stopping it. If you had unprotected sex recently, ask promptly about emergency contraception because St John's wort can also affect some emergency-contraception options. Do not stop prescribed treatment for depression without discussing a safe plan, and remember that St John's wort itself can cause important interactions with antidepressants and many other medicines.

**SOURCES:**
- UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), Drug Safety Update, *St John's wort: interactions with hormonal contraceptives*: reports reduced contraceptive effectiveness and advises women using hormonal contraception not to take St John's wort.
- Faculty of Sexual & Reproductive Healthcare (FSRH), *Drug Interactions with Hormonal Contraception*: guidance on enzyme-inducing drugs, St John's wort, contraceptive choices, backup duration, and emergency contraception.
- NCCIH, *St. John's Wort and Depression: In Depth*: St John's wort induces drug-metabolizing pathways, can weaken birth-control pills, interacts with many medicines, and can cause serious serotonin-related interactions with antidepressants.

# 5. How much zinc per day is too much?

**ANSWER:** For most adults, the tolerable upper intake level is **40 mg of elemental zinc per day**, counting food, drinks, supplements, and medicines together. That is a long-term safety ceiling, not a target; the recommended dietary amount is lower—11 mg/day for adult men and 8 mg/day for adult women, with different needs in pregnancy and breastfeeding. A clinician may prescribe more than 40 mg for a limited medical reason, but high doses should not be self-treated long term. Taking 50 mg or more for weeks can interfere with copper absorption and lead to copper deficiency, anemia, neurologic problems, and reduced immune function; nausea, vomiting, stomach upset, headache, and loss of appetite can occur sooner. Zinc also binds certain antibiotics, especially tetracyclines and quinolones, so their doses need separation. Limits for children and teenagers are lower.

**SOURCES:**
- National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements (NIH ODS), *Zinc Fact Sheet for Health Professionals*: adult recommended dietary allowances, the adult tolerable upper intake level of 40 mg/day, age-specific limits, adverse effects of excess zinc, copper deficiency, and interactions with quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics.
- National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (formerly Institute of Medicine), *Dietary Reference Intakes for Vitamin A, Vitamin K, Arsenic, Boron, Chromium, Copper, Iodine, Iron, Manganese, Molybdenum, Nickel, Silicon, Vanadium, and Zinc*: establishes zinc dietary reference intakes and tolerable upper intake levels.

# 6. Does ginkgo interact with metformin?

**ANSWER:** There is no well-established direct ginkgo–metformin interaction, but that does not prove the combination is safe. Ginkgo products have been reported to affect blood glucose in some studies, with inconsistent results, so they could complicate diabetes control even without changing metformin itself. Before combining them, ask your pharmacist or diabetes clinician and tell them the exact ginkgo product and dose. If they agree to a trial, check glucose more closely when starting, stopping, or changing ginkgo, and watch for symptoms of unusually low or high glucose. Do not replace metformin or other diabetes care with ginkgo.

**SOURCES:**
- NCCIH, *Ginkgo: Usefulness and Safety*: ginkgo can interact with medicines, evidence for its health benefits is limited, and users should discuss it with health professionals.
- The statement that no direct ginkgo–metformin interaction is well established: no source I can name.
- Reports that ginkgo may alter glucose measures, with inconsistent clinical findings: no source I can name.
- American Diabetes Association, *Standards of Care in Diabetes*, section on facilitating positive health behaviors and well-being/nutrition: dietary supplements are not recommended as a substitute for evidence-based diabetes treatment when benefit and safety are unproven.

# 7. Is ashwagandha safe with thyroid medication?

**ANSWER:** Do not combine ashwagandha with thyroid medication without the prescriber's approval. Ashwagandha may raise thyroid hormone levels, and there are case reports of thyrotoxicosis (too much thyroid hormone). With levothyroxine or another thyroid-hormone medicine, it could push the dose effect too high; with antithyroid medicine, it could work against the treatment or make control unpredictable. Ask the clinician managing your thyroid before starting it. If you already use both, tell them rather than changing thyroid medicine yourself; they may want thyroid blood tests. Seek prompt care for a fast or irregular heartbeat, chest pain, marked tremor, severe anxiety, sweating, unexplained weight loss, or confusion. Ashwagandha also has rare reports of liver injury and is not recommended during pregnancy.

**SOURCES:**
- NCCIH, *Ashwagandha: Usefulness and Safety*: ashwagandha is not recommended for people with thyroid disorders, may interact with thyroid hormone medicines, has been linked to rare liver injury, and should be avoided during pregnancy.
- National Library of Medicine, LiverTox, *Ashwagandha*: describes reported cases and the clinical pattern of ashwagandha-associated liver injury.
- Specific advice about testing and the listed urgent symptoms: based on standard clinical management of excess thyroid hormone; no single source I can name.

# 8. I take ginkgo, St John's wort, zinc and warfarin. What should I know?

**ANSWER:** This combination needs a prompt medication review—ideally today or the next business day—with the clinician or anticoagulation service that manages your warfarin.

- **Ginkgo plus warfarin:** ginkgo may impair platelet function and increase bleeding risk on top of warfarin.
- **St John's wort plus warfarin:** St John's wort induces drug-metabolizing enzymes and can reduce or destabilize warfarin's anticoagulant effect. Starting or stopping it can change the INR and clotting risk, so do not make either change without an INR plan.
- **St John's wort generally:** it interacts with many medicines, including hormonal contraceptives, antidepressants, transplant medicines, some HIV and cancer medicines, and others. A pharmacist should check your full list.
- **Zinc:** check the label for the amount of *elemental zinc* and total intake from all products. The adult upper limit is 40 mg/day unless a clinician directs otherwise. Zinc is not the main concern with warfarin, but it can interfere with absorption of tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics.

Do not stop warfarin, double or skip doses, or abruptly add or remove these supplements on your own. Bring the actual bottles or photos of the labels, including doses and how often you take them. Ask when to check your INR. Get urgent help for major or persistent bleeding, black stools, blood in vomit or urine, a sudden severe headache, one-sided weakness, chest pain, shortness of breath, a painful/swollen leg, or a significant head injury.

**SOURCES:**
- NCCIH, *Ginkgo: Usefulness and Safety*: potential increased bleeding risk with anticoagulants such as warfarin.
- EMA, Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products, *European Union herbal monograph on Ginkgo biloba L., folium* and assessment report: platelet/bleeding concerns and cautions with anticoagulants.
- FDA, *Coumadin (warfarin sodium) Prescribing Information*, drug-interaction and patient-counseling sections: CYP inducers can decrease warfarin effect, changes in interacting products require closer INR monitoring, and bleeding is the major hazard.
- NCCIH, *St. John's Wort and Depression: In Depth*: clinically important induction-based interactions with warfarin and numerous other medicines.
- NIH ODS, *Zinc Fact Sheet for Health Professionals*: 40 mg/day adult upper limit, interpretation as total elemental zinc intake, adverse effects, and antibiotic interactions.
- The statement that zinc is not the main warfarin concern in this combination: no source I can name.

# 9. I take amoxicillin. Can I take ginkgo?

**ANSWER:** No specific interaction between amoxicillin and ginkgo is well established, but supplement interactions are incompletely studied, and ginkgo can still cause side effects or interact with your other medicines. The safest approach is to ask the dispensing pharmacist before taking it and provide your complete medicine list—especially if you also use warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel, ibuprofen/naproxen, seizure medicines, or diabetes medicines. Ginkgo will not treat the infection and should not replace or delay amoxicillin. Take the antibiotic exactly as prescribed. Seek urgent help for trouble breathing, facial or throat swelling, or a severe blistering rash; contact the prescriber for severe or persistent diarrhea.

**SOURCES:**
- The statement that no specific amoxicillin–ginkgo interaction is well established: no source I can name.
- NCCIH, *Ginkgo: Usefulness and Safety*: ginkgo's adverse effects, possible bleeding risk, and potential for medicine interactions.
- U.S. National Library of Medicine, MedlinePlus, *Amoxicillin*: directions for use and warnings about serious allergic reactions and severe diarrhea.
