# Supplement and medicine questions

These are general points from published sources, not advice about any one person’s doses, labs, or other drugs. Anyone on a prescription, pregnant, or planning pregnancy should check with their own clinician or pharmacist before starting or stopping a supplement.

## 1. I take warfarin. Can I take ginkgo?

ANSWER: I would not add ginkgo while taking warfarin unless the clinician who manages the warfarin has said to. Ginkgo leaf has been tied to bleeding, including reports of bleeding in the eye and bleeding around the brain in people who were taking it. The worry with warfarin is that ginkgo may make platelets less sticky, so bleeding can rise even when the INR does not. A normal INR would not, by itself, show that the combination is safe. Standardized leaf extracts used in some trials have not always changed clotting tests, so the size of the risk at ordinary doses is still argued, but case reports plus the platelet effect are enough that anticoagulant labels and supplement monographs treat the combination as something to avoid or use only with medical supervision. Do not start or stop ginkgo on your own if you are already taking both; stopping and starting can change bleeding risk, and your warfarin dose may need a check.

SOURCES:
- Bleeding risk with ginkgo, and caution when it is combined with anticoagulants such as warfarin: National Institutes of Health, National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Ginkgo” fact sheet.
- Spontaneous bleeding around the brain in a person taking ginkgo: Rowin J, Lewis SL. Spontaneous bilateral subdural hematomas associated with chronic Ginkgo biloba ingestion. Neurology. 1996.
- Spontaneous bleeding in the eye associated with ginkgo extract: Rosenblatt M, Mindel J. Spontaneous hyphema associated with ingestion of Ginkgo biloba extract. New England Journal of Medicine. 1997.
- Platelet-activating factor blockade as the proposed reason platelets are affected: Braquet P and related work on ginkgolide B (BN 52021) as a platelet-activating factor antagonist (see question 3).
- A short course of the standardized extract EGb 761 did not change bleeding time or standard coagulation tests in healthy volunteers: Köhler S, Funk P, Kieser M. Influence of a 7-day treatment with Ginkgo biloba special extract EGb 761 on bleeding time and coagulation. Blood Coagulation & Fibrinolysis. 2004.
- How strongly those threads should be weighed for a person already on warfarin: no source I can name beyond the documents above; that weighing is a clinical judgment.

## 2. Is ginkgo safe during pregnancy?

ANSWER: There is not enough reliable evidence to call ginkgo safe in pregnancy, and the usual advice is not to take it. Leaf extracts may reduce how well platelets clump, which is a particular concern around delivery when bleeding matters. Ginkgo seed is a different product from the leaf extract sold for memory and should not be used in pregnancy; the seed can cause serious poisoning. Safety data for the leaf in pregnancy are too thin to set a dose that is known to be safe for the pregnant person or the fetus. Someone who is pregnant or trying to conceive should stop ginkgo unless their obstetric clinician tells them otherwise.

SOURCES:
- Not enough information to judge safety in pregnancy, and a bleeding concern with ginkgo: National Institutes of Health, National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Ginkgo” fact sheet.
- A specific pregnancy study, a safe dose, or an official statement that leaf extract causes birth defects: no source I can name.
- Ginkgo seed poisoning from ginkgotoxin (4'-O-methylpyridoxine), including seizures: food-poisoning reports and reviews of Ginkgo biloba seed; no single document title I can name with confidence here.

## 3. What does ginkgo do to blood clotting, and how?

ANSWER: The part of ginkgo leaf that is discussed for clotting is a group of terpenes called ginkgolides, especially ginkgolide B. Platelet-activating factor is a signal that helps platelets clump. Ginkgolide B blocks the platelet-activating factor receptor, so in lab systems it can reduce that kind of platelet clumping. Supplements may also contain flavone glycosides; those are not the main proposed antiplatelet compounds. In people, the picture is weaker than the lab story. A standard extract (often EGb 761) at usual doses has, in at least one short study, left bleeding time and common clotting tests unchanged. Serious bleeding has still been reported in people taking ginkgo, usually as single cases rather than as a measured effect in a trial. So the mechanism is “less platelet clumping via platelet-activating factor blockade,” and the practical size of that effect at supplement doses is uncertain. This is a platelet effect, not the same as warfarin’s effect on clotting-factor production, which is why the INR can stay steady while bleeding risk still changes.

SOURCES:
- Ginkgolide B as a platelet-activating factor antagonist: Braquet P. Work on BN 52021 (ginkgolide B), including reports in the prostaglandin and thromboxane research literature in the mid-1980s. No single page citation I can name more tightly than that.
- No change in bleeding time or coagulation tests after 7 days of EGb 761 in healthy people: Köhler S, Funk P, Kieser M. Blood Coagulation & Fibrinolysis. 2004.
- Human bleeding cases that keep the clinical concern alive: Rowin and Lewis, Neurology, 1996; Rosenblatt and Mindel, New England Journal of Medicine, 1997.
- A definitive trial that measures how much ordinary ginkgo doses change clot strength in people on no other blood-thinning drug: no source I can name.

## 4. I'm on birth control pills. Does St John's wort matter?

ANSWER: Yes. St John’s wort can make combined birth control pills less effective. It speeds the liver’s breakdown of the pill’s hormones, so hormone levels fall. People have had breakthrough bleeding and pregnancies while taking both. The herb does this mainly through hyperforin, which switches on a receptor called the pregnane X receptor and raises the drug-metabolizing enzyme CYP3A4; the pill’s estrogen and progestin are substrates of that enzyme. The interaction can last after the herb is stopped, because the enzyme increase takes time to wear off. This is not a reason to stop a prescribed pill abruptly. It is a reason not to take St John’s wort with oral contraceptives, and to use a backup method and contact the prescriber if both have already been taken. The same induction can affect other hormonal methods that contain ethinyl estradiol or similar steroids; a pharmacist should check the specific product.

SOURCES:
- Unintended pregnancies and breakthrough bleeding reported with St John’s wort plus oral contraceptives, and regulatory advice that the herb reduces contraceptive effectiveness: United Kingdom Committee on Safety of Medicines / Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency warning on St John’s wort, 2000.
- United States regulatory notice that St John’s wort interacts with many drugs, including oral contraceptives: U.S. Food and Drug Administration public health advisory on St John’s wort, 2000.
- Lower oral-contraceptive exposure and breakthrough bleeding in a clinical study: Hall SD, Wang Z, Huang SM, et al. The interaction between St John’s wort and an oral contraceptive. Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics. 2003.
- Further pharmacokinetic and bleeding findings with a contraceptive: Murphy PA, Kern SE, Stanczyk FZ, Westhoff CL. Interaction of St. John’s Wort with oral contraceptives. Contraception. 2005.
- Hyperforin activates the pregnane X receptor and induces CYP3A4: Moore LB, Goodwin B, Jones SA, et al. St. John’s wort induces hepatic drug metabolism through activation of the pregnane X receptor. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the USA. 2000.

## 5. How much zinc per day is too much?

ANSWER: For adults, the tolerable upper intake level is 40 milligrams of zinc a day. That figure is total zinc from food and supplements together, not 40 milligrams on top of diet. Intakes above that, especially if they go on for weeks or months, can cause nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain in the short term, and can block copper absorption if they continue. Long-standing copper deficiency from extra zinc can cause anemia and nerve problems. The 40-milligram cap is for adults 19 and older; infants, children, and teens have lower caps. Zinc in a normal diet is not the problem. A high-dose lozenge, “immune” tablet, or several products taken together can pass 40 milligrams quickly. People who take zinc for a long time at high doses are sometimes told to watch copper status. Pregnant people have their own recommended intakes and should not use the adult upper level as a personal target without obstetric advice.

SOURCES:
- Adult tolerable upper intake level of 40 mg/day of zinc, set because higher chronic intakes impair copper status: Institute of Medicine, Food and Nutrition Board. Dietary Reference Intakes for Vitamin A, Vitamin K, Arsenic, Boron, Chromium, Copper, Iodine, Iron, Manganese, Molybdenum, Nickel, Silicon, Vanadium, and Zinc. National Academies Press. 2001.
- The same 40 mg/day adult upper level, and the copper-deficiency and gastrointestinal effects of excess zinc: National Institutes of Health, Office of Dietary Supplements, Zinc Fact Sheet for Health Professionals.

## 6. Does ginkgo interact with metformin?

ANSWER: I cannot point to a well-established interaction in which ginkgo reliably raises or lowers the amount of metformin in the blood. Metformin is cleared largely by the kidneys, and ginkgo is not a proven cause of metformin toxicity or failure. Separate from that, ginkgo has been studied for effects on insulin and blood glucose, and those study results are mixed, so a change in glucose is possible even without a classic drug-level interaction. Someone on metformin who starts or stops ginkgo should watch glucose more closely and tell the clinician managing the diabetes. This pairing is not in the same class as warfarin plus ginkgo, or birth control pills plus St John’s wort, where the interaction itself is the reason to avoid the combination.

SOURCES:
- Studies of ginkgo extract on insulin and glucose handling, including work in people with type 2 diabetes: Kudolo GB and colleagues, Journal of Clinical Pharmacology, around 2000–2001. I cannot name the exact article title and year with confidence.
- A clinical study that measures a meaningful change in metformin concentrations or metformin effect when ginkgo is added: no source I can name.
- Renal elimination of metformin as the reason a liver-enzyme herb is a less likely partner: metformin prescribing information (U.S. Food and Drug Administration label) describes renal clearance. An interaction monograph that then rules ginkgo in or out: no source I can name.

## 7. Is ashwagandha safe with thyroid medication?

ANSWER: Not enough to assume it is safe. Ashwagandha can raise thyroid hormone levels. In animals it increased thyroid hormone, and in a small trial in people with subclinical hypothyroidism it raised thyroxine and lowered TSH. Thyroid medication such as levothyroxine is prescribed to hit a target TSH. Adding something that also raises thyroid hormone can push a person toward too much hormone: palpitations, tremor, heat intolerance, weight loss, and an abnormal TSH. There are also reports of thyroid overactivity in people taking ashwagandha who were not necessarily on thyroid medicine. Someone already on levothyroxine, liothyronine, or a desiccated thyroid product should not add ashwagandha unless the prescriber agrees and plans to recheck TSH. If both are already being taken, that person should tell the prescriber rather than stopping the prescription hormone on their own.

SOURCES:
- Ashwagandha root extract increased serum T4 and decreased TSH in adults with subclinical hypothyroidism: Sharma AK, Basu I, Singh S. Efficacy and Safety of Ashwagandha Root Extract in Subclinical Hypothyroid Patients: A Double-Blind, Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial. Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine. 2018.
- Increased thyroid hormone concentrations after ashwagandha root extract in an animal study: Panda S, Kar A. Journal of Pharmacy and Pharmacology. 1998. I am confident this finding is widely cited; I cannot restate the article title with certainty.
- Case reports of thyrotoxicosis linked to ashwagandha: no source I can name precisely.
- A trial of ashwagandha added to a stable dose of levothyroxine: no source I can name.

## 8. I take ginkgo, St John's wort, zinc and warfarin. What should I know?

ANSWER: This combination has two different problems with warfarin, and they do not cancel each other. St John’s wort induces the enzymes that clear warfarin, especially CYP2C9 for the more active S-warfarin, and it can lower the INR and weaken clot protection. That effect was shown in healthy volunteers: warfarin was cleared faster and the INR fell. If St John’s wort is stopped later, enzyme activity falls and the INR can rise, so the warfarin dose that was safe on the herb can become too high. Ginkgo is the other direction: it may reduce platelet clumping and has been linked to bleeding, and that risk is not measured by the INR. Taking both herbs means the INR can look low or “fine” while platelets are still being affected, or the INR can swing when the wort is started or stopped. Zinc is a separate issue. The adult upper level is 40 milligrams a day from food plus supplements; more than that for a long time can cause copper deficiency. I cannot name a established direct interaction in which ordinary zinc doses change warfarin’s INR. Do not stop warfarin, and do not stop or start either herb, without the service that monitors the INR. They need to know all four products, check the INR on a schedule they choose, and look for bleeding (bruises, nosebleeds, black stools, severe headache). Zinc dose should be added up across every product so it is not over 40 milligrams a day unless a clinician has prescribed more.

SOURCES:
- St John’s wort increased warfarin clearance and reduced the INR in healthy subjects: Jiang X, Williams KM, Liauw WS, et al. Effect of St John’s wort and ginseng on the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of warfarin in healthy subjects. British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology. 2004.
- S-warfarin is metabolized by CYP2C9, and St John’s wort induces drug-metabolizing enzymes through the pregnane X receptor: warfarin prescribing information (U.S. Food and Drug Administration label) for the CYP2C9 pathway; Moore LB et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the USA, 2000, for pregnane X receptor induction. A single paper that measures CYP2C9 induction by St John’s wort in the liver of people on warfarin: the Jiang 2004 trial above is the clinical warfarin evidence I can name.
- Regulatory warnings that St John’s wort interacts with warfarin: United Kingdom Committee on Safety of Medicines / Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, 2000; U.S. Food and Drug Administration public health advisory on St John’s wort, 2000.
- Ginkgo, bleeding, and anticoagulants: National Institutes of Health, National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Ginkgo” fact sheet; Rowin and Lewis, Neurology, 1996; Rosenblatt and Mindel, New England Journal of Medicine, 1997.
- Zinc upper level 40 mg/day: Institute of Medicine Dietary Reference Intakes, 2001; National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements Zinc Fact Sheet.
- A documented zinc–warfarin interaction that changes the INR: no source I can name.

## 9. I take amoxicillin. Can I take ginkgo?

ANSWER: I cannot name a known interaction in which ginkgo changes how amoxicillin works or how the body clears it. Amoxicillin is removed mainly by the kidneys as unchanged drug, not by the liver enzymes herbs often affect. Ginkgo’s separate bleeding concern still applies if the person also takes warfarin, aspirin, or another blood-thinning drug, or if they are heading into surgery, but amoxicillin itself is not a blood thinner. For a short course of amoxicillin in someone who is not on an anticoagulant and has no bleeding disorder, ginkgo is not a combination I would flag the way I would flag ginkgo with warfarin. That is not a promise that every ginkgo product is safe; products vary, and anyone who has had unusual bleeding should ask a pharmacist before continuing it.

SOURCES:
- Amoxicillin is excreted mainly unchanged in urine: amoxicillin prescribing information, U.S. Food and Drug Administration label.
- A study, label, or interaction monograph showing that ginkgo alters amoxicillin levels or effect: no source I can name.
- Ginkgo and bleeding when anticoagulants are also in use: National Institutes of Health, National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Ginkgo” fact sheet (relevant only if a bleeding-risk drug is also being taken).
