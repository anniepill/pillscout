# 1. I take warfarin. Can I take ginkgo?

ANSWER:

This record does not give a single yes or no. It returns several statements about ginkgo and warfarin, and they do not all say the same thing. Here is what they say, grouped by how the lookup matched them.

**The source names warfarin.**

In the well-established-use material of EMA/HMPC/321097/2012, the interactions section says that if the product is taken at the same time as anticoagulants (the examples given are phenprocoumon and warfarin) or antiplatelet drugs (the examples given are clopidogrel, acetylsalicylic acid, and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), their effect may be influenced.

The same section says available studies with warfarin do not indicate an interaction between warfarin and Ginkgo biloba products. It advises adequate monitoring when you start Ginkgo biloba, when you change the dose, when you stop it, or if you change product. That statement is also in the well-established-use material.

A study summary in EMA/HMPC/321095/2012 (Jiang et al. 2005) says the international normalised ratio of prothrombine time and platelet aggregation were not affected by Ginkgo biloba taken together with warfarin. It says that EGb 761 at recommended doses does not significantly affect clotting status or the pharmacodynamics of warfarin in healthy subjects.

A PubMed abstract filed with this named match says INR and platelet aggregation were not affected by administration of ginkgo or ginger alone.

The NCCIH safety section says ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding in people who are taking anticoagulant drugs, such as warfarin.

The FDA label for JANTOVEN says some botanicals may cause bleeding events when taken alone (the examples are garlic and Ginkgo biloba) and may have anticoagulant, antiplatelet, and/or fibrinolytic properties. It says these effects would be expected to be additive to the anticoagulant effects of JANTOVEN. The lookup says JANTOVEN is a warfarin product and that it made this match.

The same label says some botanicals and foods can interact with JANTOVEN through CYP450 interactions. The examples are echinacea, grapefruit juice, ginkgo, goldenseal, and St. John's wort. The lookup again says JANTOVEN is a warfarin product.

**The passage names warfarin, and the record files it under a different heading.**

A laboratory passage in EMA/HMPC/321095/2012 (Von Moltke et al. 2004) says quercetin was a strong inhibitor of CYP2C9 (IC50 7.8 microg/ml, 25.8 microM), and that a number of flavonol aglycones, as well as the biflavonol amentoflavone, inhibit several human CYP enzymes in vitro. It says inhibition of CYP2C9 is of potential concern because that enzyme clears some drugs, such as S-warfarin, that have narrow therapeutic ranges, and that the importance of these laboratory findings requires evaluation in clinical studies. The passage does not name a ginkgo product. The file's note says the passage names the item and is filed under a different heading.

A PubMed passage filed the same way reports the mean ratio (90% CI) for the S-7-hydroxywarfarin urinary excretion rate as 1.07 (0.85–1.32) for ginkgo treatment, and 1.00 (0.81–1.23) for ginger. It says these herbs did not affect CYP2C9 activity.

**The source named a class. The lookup matched warfarin to that class. The source did not name warfarin.**

The class named for these three statements is anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs.

The Health Canada ginkgo monograph says: do not use if you are taking blood thinners or other health products that affect blood coagulation. The record marks this as a contraindication.

EMA/HMPC/321097/2012, in its traditional-use material, says ginkgo leaf may increase the effects of anticoagulants such as coumarin derivatives.

The same monograph, in its well-established-use material, says that in patients with a pathologically increased bleeding tendency (haemorrhagic diathesis) and concomitant anticoagulant and antiplatelet treatment, the product should only be used after consultation with a doctor. The record marks this as ask-before-use. It applies to patients with haemorrhagic diathesis or concomitant anticoagulant or antiplatelet treatment.

**The source named an enzyme pathway. The lookup matched warfarin to that pathway. The source did not name warfarin.**

A laboratory passage in EMA/HMPC/321095/2012 (Gaudineau et al. 2004) says EGb 761 inhibited all the CYP enzymes studied except CYP2D6 (Ki greater than 900 microg/ml). CYP2C9 was the most affected (Ki 14 plus or minus 4 microg/ml). CYP1A2 (106 plus or minus 24 microg/ml), CYP2E1 (127 plus or minus 42 microg/ml), and CYP3A4 (155 plus or minus 43 microg/ml) were also inhibited, to a lesser extent.

The clinical-pharmacology conclusions in the same monograph say the influence of Ginkgo biloba on drug-metabolising CYPs has been shown with probe substrates. For CYP3A4, interaction studies suggested both an inducing effect and an inhibitory effect, depending on the study design. A daily dose of 240 mg of EGb 761, and extracts other than EGb 761, were described as inducing. A higher daily dose of EGb 761 was described as inhibitory. An induction of CYP2C9 was assumed at a daily dose of 360 mg of EGb 761. Inducing effects on CYP2C19 were also described for Ginkgo biloba extracts other than EGb 761. The record attaches this statement to a dose at or above 240 mg.

# 3. I take ginkgo, St John's wort, zinc and warfarin. What should I know?

ANSWER:

The file reports these as separate pairs. It has no extra statement about all four taken together. Several pairs come back empty, and the file says an empty result is not a finding of safety. Here is each pair.

**Ginkgo with warfarin**

This pair has the same kinds of statements as a ginkgo-and-warfarin lookup.

Where the source names warfarin: In the well-established-use material of EMA/HMPC/321097/2012, if the product is taken with anticoagulants (examples include warfarin) or antiplatelet drugs, their effect may be influenced. Available studies with warfarin do not indicate an interaction between warfarin and Ginkgo biloba products, but adequate monitoring is advised when starting Ginkgo biloba, changing the dose, stopping it, or changing product. A study summary (Jiang et al. 2005) says the international normalised ratio of prothrombine time and platelet aggregation were not affected by Ginkgo biloba coadministration, and that EGb 761 at recommended doses does not significantly affect clotting status or the pharmacodynamics of warfarin in healthy subjects. A PubMed abstract says INR and platelet aggregation were not affected by ginkgo or ginger alone. The NCCIH safety section says ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding in people taking anticoagulant drugs such as warfarin. The JANTOVEN label, which the lookup identifies as a warfarin product, says some botanicals may cause bleeding when taken alone (examples include Ginkgo biloba) and may have anticoagulant, antiplatelet, and/or fibrinolytic properties, and that these effects would be expected to be additive to JANTOVEN. The same label says some botanicals and foods can interact with JANTOVEN through CYP450 interactions, and it lists ginkgo among the examples.

Where the passage names the item and is filed under a different heading: a laboratory passage says quercetin strongly inhibited CYP2C9 and that flavonol aglycones and amentoflavone inhibit several human CYP enzymes in vitro. A separate passage says the S-7-hydroxywarfarin urinary excretion ratio for ginkgo was 1.07 (0.85–1.32) and that ginkgo and ginger did not affect CYP2C9 activity.

Where the source named the class "anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs," and the lookup matched warfarin to that class without the source naming warfarin: the Health Canada ginkgo monograph says do not use ginkgo if you are taking blood thinners or other products that affect blood coagulation, and the record marks that as a contraindication. Where the source named an enzyme pathway and the lookup matched warfarin to it, without naming warfarin: EGb 761 inhibited the CYP enzymes studied except CYP2D6, and CYP2C9 was the most affected. The clinical-pharmacology summary says Ginkgo biloba can induce or inhibit CYP3A4 depending on the study, describes induction at 240 mg a day of EGb 761 and with other extracts, describes inhibition at a higher daily dose of EGb 761, assumes CYP2C9 induction at 360 mg a day of EGb 761, and describes CYP2C19 induction for extracts other than EGb 761. The record attaches that summary to a dose at or above 240 mg.

**St John's wort with warfarin**

Nothing in this pair names warfarin. Every match is a class or an enzyme pathway that the lookup connected to warfarin.

The contraindications section of EMA/HMPC/7695/2021 lists concomitant use with coumarin-type anticoagulants, and also lists cyclosporine, everolimus, sirolimus, tacrolimus for systemic use, fosamprenavir, indinavir and other protease inhibitors, nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, irinotecan, imatinib, and other cytostatic agents metabolised by CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19 or transported by P-glycoprotein. The record marks this as a contraindication. The lookup matched warfarin to that class; the source did not name warfarin. The record limits this statement to a hyperforin daily dose above 1 mg, for well-established use and the traditional-use high-dose tier.

On enzyme pathways, the same monograph says that when daily hyperforin intake is less than 1 mg and use is not longer than 2 weeks, no clinically relevant interactions are reported for drugs metabolised via CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4 or transported by P-glycoprotein. The record's dose tiers mark hyperforin at or below 1 mg a day as no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interactions reported, and hyperforin above 1 mg a day as the full induction profile applying.

Two pharmacokinetic passages say hyperforin induces CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and P-glycoprotein dose-dependently through the PXR system. One of them adds that elimination of other drug substances may be accelerated, resulting in decreased plasma concentrations. The record attaches these to a hyperforin daily dose above 1 mg.

The interactions section says special care should be taken with drugs whose metabolism is influenced by CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, or P-glycoprotein, because a reduction of plasma concentrations is possible. The examples named are amitriptyline, fexofenadine, alprazolam, diazepam, midazolam, methadone, simvastatin, digoxin, and finasteride. The record gives this statement with no dose condition. The lookup matched it by enzyme pathway; the source did not name warfarin.
