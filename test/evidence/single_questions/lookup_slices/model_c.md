# Question 1: I take warfarin. Can I take ginkgo?

ANSWER:
Based on the medical lookup records, you should exercise great caution, and health authorities advise against taking ginkgo with warfarin without medical supervision. The records show conflicting evidence across regulatory warnings, clinical trials, and laboratory studies:

1. Warnings and Contraindications:
- Health Canada lists a direct contraindication: do not use ginkgo if you are taking blood thinners or other health products that affect blood coagulation.
- The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) warns that ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding in people taking anticoagulant medications such as warfarin.
- FDA labeling for warfarin (Jantoven) notes that botanicals like Ginkgo biloba may cause bleeding events on their own and may possess anticoagulant, antiplatelet, or fibrinolytic properties, which would be expected to be additive to warfarin's blood-thinning effects. It also notes potential interactions via cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme pathways.
- European regulatory guidelines (EMA/HMPC) note that ginkgo leaf may increase or influence the effect of anticoagulants (such as coumarin derivatives/warfarin). They state that patients with an increased bleeding tendency who are taking anticoagulants should only use ginkgo after consulting a doctor.

2. Clinical Trials in Healthy Subjects:
- Clinical studies evaluating ginkgo co-administration with warfarin found that ginkgo (specifically extract EGb 761 at recommended doses) did not significantly affect clotting status, prothrombin time (INR), or platelet aggregation in healthy individuals. Similarly, ginkgo treatment alone did not affect INR, platelet aggregation, or CYP2C9 activity (as measured by urinary excretion rates).
- EMA records state that available studies do not indicate a direct interaction between warfarin and Ginkgo biloba products. However, they explicitly advise that adequate monitoring is needed when starting, stopping, changing the dose of ginkgo, or switching ginkgo products.

3. Laboratory and Metabolism Findings:
- In laboratory (in vitro) research, ginkgo constituents (such as quercetin and flavonol aglycones) and ginkgo extract (EGb 761) strongly inhibited CYP2C9, the primary enzyme responsible for clearing S-warfarin. Because warfarin has a narrow therapeutic range, CYP2C9 inhibition could theoretically increase drug levels, though clinical trials are required to evaluate in vitro findings. Additionally, high daily doses of EGb 761 (360 mg) suggested potential CYP2C9 induction.

Summary:
Because major health authorities advise against combining them or warn of additive bleeding risks, you should consult your healthcare provider before taking ginkgo. If taken, close monitoring of your blood clotting status (INR) is recommended when starting, stopping, or adjusting ginkgo intake.

# Question 3: I take ginkgo, St John's wort, zinc and warfarin. What should I know?

ANSWER:
Taking this combination involves serious drug-herb interactions, official contraindications, and specific timing requirements. Based strictly on the provided lookup records, here is what you need to know:

1. St. John's Wort with Warfarin:
- Direct Contraindication: European regulatory guidelines (EMA/HMPC) state that concomitant use of St. John's wort with coumarin-type anticoagulants (which includes warfarin) is contraindicated when the daily intake of hyperforin exceeds 1 mg.
- Reduced Warfarin Effectiveness: Hyperforin dose-dependently induces metabolic enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19) and the transporter P-glycoprotein via the PXR system. This accelerates the elimination of co-administered drugs metabolized by these enzymes, decreasing their plasma concentrations. Accelerating warfarin clearance can lower blood levels of the drug, potentially compromising anticoagulation and increasing clot risk. Special care is advised for any drugs metabolized by these enzymes.
- Dose Thresholds: If hyperforin intake is less than 1 mg daily and taken for no longer than 2 weeks, no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interactions have been reported. Above 1 mg daily, induction occurs and use is contraindicated.
- FDA labeling for warfarin (Jantoven) also identifies St. John's wort as interacting through CYP450 enzymes.

2. Ginkgo with Warfarin:
- Bleeding Risk & Contraindications: Health Canada states that ginkgo is contraindicated with blood thinners ("Do not use if you are taking blood thinners or other health products that affect blood coagulation"). The NCCIH notes that ginkgo may increase bleeding risk in people taking anticoagulants like warfarin. FDA labeling warns that botanicals like Ginkgo biloba may cause bleeding events alone, possess anticoagulant/antiplatelet properties that could have additive effects with warfarin, and interact through CYP450 pathways. EMA guidelines state that ginkgo leaf may increase or influence anticoagulant effects and advise that patients with bleeding tendencies taking anticoagulants should only use ginkgo after consulting a physician.
- Conflicting Clinical Evidence: In clinical trials with healthy subjects, standardized ginkgo extract (EGb 761 at recommended doses) did not alter INR, clotting status, platelet aggregation, or CYP2C9 activity. EMA notes available clinical trials do not show a direct interaction with warfarin, but strongly advises adequate monitoring whenever starting, stopping, changing doses, or changing ginkgo products. In vitro studies show inhibition of CYP2C9 (which clears S-warfarin), and high doses (360 mg) suggested CYP2C9 induction.

3. Zinc with Warfarin and Other Supplements:
- Dosing and Timing: The Health Canada monograph for products providing zinc (along with calcium or iron) directs that they must be taken with food, and must be taken a few hours before or after taking other medications or health products. This means zinc must not be taken at the same time as warfarin, ginkgo, or St. John's wort.

4. Interactions Between the Supplements:
- Ginkgo and St. John's Wort: FDA labeling indicates both botanicals can interact with warfarin through CYP450 interactions. The St. John's wort record contains no statement naming ginkgo.
- Ginkgo, St. John's Wort, and Zinc: Individual supplement records for ginkgo and St. John's wort contain no statements naming zinc.
- The lookup records explicitly note for each of these unlisted pairs that the absence of a statement naming a product or class is not a finding of safety. Furthermore, the rule to take zinc a few hours apart applies to all oral medications and health products.

Summary:
You should not take this combination without consulting your healthcare provider. St. John's wort is contraindicated with warfarin at standard hyperforin doses (>1 mg/day) because it can reduce warfarin levels. Ginkgo carries significant warnings regarding increased bleeding risk and additive anticoagulant effects, requiring close monitoring if used. Zinc must be taken with food and separated by a few hours from all your other medications and supplements.
